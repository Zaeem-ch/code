What Is a Mansard Loft Conversion?

A mansard loft conversion is the most transformative and space-efficient type of roof conversion available to London homeowners. Named after the 17th-century French architect François Mansart, who popularised the style in Parisian architecture, a mansard conversion involves rebuilding one or both slopes of the existing roof to create near-vertical walls at approximately 72 degrees from the horizontal, topped by a flat or very gently sloping roof section. This distinctive profile creates a full-height room with almost no wasted space from sloping ceilings, effectively adding a complete new storey to the property.

The mansard approach is fundamentally different from other loft conversion types. Where a dormer conversion adds a box-shaped projection to an otherwise unchanged roof, and a hip to gable conversion modifies just the end wall, a mansard rebuilds the entire rear roof slope (and sometimes the front as well) to maximise the internal volume. The result is a living space that feels like a purpose-built room rather than a converted attic, with generous headroom throughout and vertical walls that allow standard furniture placement right up to the perimeter.

Why Mansard Conversions Require Full Planning Permission

Unlike rear dormers and some hip to gable conversions, a mansard loft conversion always requires full planning permission. This is because the works fundamentally alter the shape, profile, and appearance of the roof, which falls outside the scope of permitted development rights. The relevant legislation, Schedule 2, Part 1, Class B of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015, only permits roof enlargements that do not materially alter the shape of the dwellinghouse. A mansard conversion clearly does alter the shape, making a planning application unavoidable.

The requirement for planning permission should not be seen as a barrier. Many London boroughs have well-established policies and design precedents for mansard conversions, particularly in areas dominated by Victorian and Edwardian terraced housing. Boroughs such as Hammersmith and Fulham, Wandsworth, Lambeth, and Islington regularly approve mansard applications where the design is sympathetic to the existing streetscape and complies with local planning policies.

The current householder planning application fee is £258 (as of 2024), and the statutory determination period is 8 weeks from the date of validation. We strongly recommend engaging in pre-application discussions with the planning authority before submitting a formal application. The pre-application service (typically £150-£300 depending on the borough) provides an informal opinion from a planning officer on the likelihood of the proposal being approved, along with guidance on any design modifications that would improve the chances of success.

Mansard Conversions and London Properties

The mansard conversion has become synonymous with London's residential architecture, and for good reason. The capital's housing stock is dominated by Victorian and Edwardian terraced houses, many of which have relatively shallow roof pitches that limit the scope for other conversion types. The terraced form, with party walls on both sides and a continuous rear roof line visible only from the gardens, makes mansard conversions particularly appropriate because the altered roof profile does not affect the front elevation or the public streetscape.

In inner London boroughs, where land values are highest and the demand for additional living space is most intense, the mansard conversion offers the greatest return on investment. A well-designed mansard can add 30 to 50 square metres of habitable floor space to a typical Victorian terrace, creating enough room for a large master bedroom with en-suite, a second bedroom, and potentially a study or dressing area. In boroughs such as Kensington and Westminster, where property values exceed £1,000 per square foot, the value added by a mansard conversion can substantially exceed its cost.

The design of a London mansard must respond to the specific character of the street and the surrounding buildings. Planning officers will assess whether the proposed mansard is consistent with the prevailing roofscape, the height of adjacent buildings, and any established pattern of mansard conversions in the area. Where neighbours have already carried out mansard conversions, this creates a precedent that strengthens subsequent applications. Our design approach takes account of these contextual factors, ensuring that each mansard we design sits comfortably within its setting and addresses the specific policies of the relevant borough.

The 72-Degree Wall: Mansard Design Principles

The defining feature of a mansard conversion is the steep, near-vertical wall that replaces the existing roof slope. This wall is typically set at 72 degrees from the horizontal, an angle that has become the standard across most London boroughs. The 72-degree angle represents a carefully considered compromise between maximising the internal volume (a fully vertical wall would create the most space) and maintaining a recognisable mansard profile that is visually distinct from a full additional storey.

The mansard wall is constructed as a structural timber frame clad externally with hanging slates or tiles to match or complement the materials used on the original roof. The framing consists of inclined studs at 400mm or 600mm centres, with horizontal noggins providing lateral bracing. Insulation, typically mineral wool or rigid PIR boards, is fitted between and sometimes over the studs to achieve the required U-value of 0.18 W/m2K. A vapour control layer on the warm side of the insulation and a breather membrane on the cold side manage moisture movement and prevent condensation within the construction.

The flat roof section at the top of the mansard is typically constructed as a warm roof, with insulation above the structural deck and a single-ply membrane, GRP fibreglass, or zinc standing seam finish. The fall on the flat section should be a minimum of 1:40 (preferably 1:60) to ensure rainwater drains effectively to the gutters. Our drawings detail the complete build-up of the mansard wall and flat roof, including every layer from the internal plasterboard finish through to the external slate or tile hanging.

The Planning Application Process for Mansard Conversions

Preparing a successful planning application for a mansard conversion requires careful attention to both the design quality and the documentation submitted. At ProStruct Design, we manage the entire planning process on behalf of our clients, from the initial survey through to the receipt of the decision notice.

Pre-Application Stage

Before investing in a full set of planning drawings, we recommend a pre-application enquiry with the local planning authority. This involves submitting outline drawings and a brief description of the proposed works to the council, who will provide written feedback on the acceptability of the scheme. The pre-application fee varies by borough but is typically between £150 and £300 for a householder enquiry. The response, usually received within 4 to 6 weeks, gives a strong indication of whether the proposal will be supported and highlights any design changes needed.

Drawing Preparation

The planning drawing package for a mansard conversion includes a site location plan at 1:1250 scale showing the property in its wider context, a block plan at 1:500 showing the property boundary and surrounding buildings, existing and proposed floor plans at 1:50 scale, existing and proposed elevations at 1:100 (all four elevations), existing and proposed roof plans, and at least one cross-section through the mansard at 1:50 showing the internal room layout and the relationship between the new and existing roof structures.

Supporting Documents

A design and access statement is required, explaining the design rationale and demonstrating how the proposal responds to the local context. Photographs of the existing property and the surrounding streetscape are included to help the planning officer visualise the site. In sensitive areas near listed buildings, a heritage statement may also be required. We prepare all of these documents as part of our standard service.

Submission and Determination

Applications are submitted online through the Planning Portal, and the council validates the submission within 1 to 2 weeks. The 8-week determination period runs from validation. During this time, the council will consult neighbours and any relevant statutory consultees. The planning officer may request amendments or additional information, which we handle directly. If the application is approved, the decision notice will set out any conditions that must be complied with during construction.

Structural Engineering for Mansard Conversions

The structural engineering demands of a mansard conversion are more extensive than any other loft conversion type. The existing rear roof slope is entirely removed and replaced with the new mansard structure, which means the roof must be temporarily supported while the works are carried out. The new mansard walls must be designed to resist wind loads, support the flat roof structure, and transfer all loads safely down through the existing building to the foundations.

Steel beams are used extensively in mansard conversions. A primary beam typically spans across the property at the junction between the existing front roof slope and the new mansard, supporting both the retained front rafters and the new mansard frame. Additional beams support the flat roof, form the head of the mansard wall, and carry the new loft floor. The connection details between steelwork and the existing masonry walls are critical, and our structural drawings show padstone sizes, bolt specifications, and bearing lengths for every connection.

The floor structure must be upgraded to support habitable room loads. Existing ceiling joists in Victorian and Edwardian houses are typically 175mm x 50mm at 400mm centres, which is inadequate for a bedroom floor. New engineered timber joists (typically 225mm or 250mm deep) or steel beams are installed to provide the necessary load-bearing capacity. Our structural calculations demonstrate compliance with deflection limits, vibration criteria, and ultimate load-bearing requirements.

Costs for Mansard Loft Conversions

Mansard loft conversions are the most expensive type of loft conversion, reflecting the complexity of the structural work, the need for full planning permission, and the high-quality finishes typically specified. In London, costs range from £45,000 to £80,000, with the final figure depending on the property size, the specification level, and the extent of the works.

A rear mansard on a standard Victorian mid-terrace house in an outer London borough, with a single bedroom and en-suite bathroom at a mid-range specification, will typically cost between £45,000 and £55,000. A larger scheme incorporating two bedrooms, a higher specification bathroom, and premium finishes will fall in the £55,000 to £70,000 range. In premium central London boroughs where specification expectations are higher and access is more challenging, costs can reach £70,000 to £80,000 or more.

Professional fees for the architectural drawings, structural engineering, and planning application preparation typically range from £4,000 to £6,000 for a mansard conversion. The planning application fee (currently £258 for householder applications) and building control fees (£400-£800 depending on the borough) are additional costs payable directly to the local authority. We provide a clear, itemised fee proposal at the outset so there are no surprises during the project.

Frequently Asked Questions About Mansard Loft Conversions

Does a mansard loft conversion always need planning permission?

Yes, a mansard loft conversion always requires full planning permission because it fundamentally alters the shape and profile of the roof. Unlike rear dormers or hip to gable conversions, mansard extensions cannot be carried out under permitted development rights. A householder planning application must be submitted to the local council, with the current fee being £258. The determination period is typically 8 weeks from validation.

How much does a mansard loft conversion cost in London?

Mansard loft conversions in London typically cost between £45,000 and £80,000, making them the most expensive type of loft conversion. The higher cost reflects the extensive structural work required, including rebuilding the rear roof slope, constructing the near-vertical mansard walls, and the need for full planning permission. The range depends on the property size, specification level, and borough-specific requirements.

What angle are mansard walls built at?

Mansard walls are typically built at an angle of 72 degrees from the horizontal, which is the standard angle accepted by most London borough planning departments. This steep angle means the walls are nearly vertical, providing maximum usable floor space while maintaining the distinctive mansard profile. Some boroughs may accept slightly different angles depending on the local context and the character of surrounding buildings.

Why are mansard conversions so popular in London?

Mansard conversions are particularly popular in London because they offer the greatest volume gain of any loft conversion type, which is especially valuable given London's high property values. They are ideal for terraced Victorian and Edwardian houses, which make up a large proportion of London's housing stock. Many London boroughs have established design precedents for mansards, making planning approval more achievable when the design follows local guidelines.

What architectural drawings are needed for a mansard planning application?

A mansard planning application requires a site location plan at 1:1250 scale, a block plan at 1:500, existing and proposed floor plans at 1:50, existing and proposed elevations at 1:100, existing and proposed roof plans, cross-sections through the mansard, a design and access statement, and photographs showing the existing property and its context within the street. Some boroughs may also require a heritage statement if the property is near listed buildings.

How long does the planning process take for a mansard conversion?

The statutory determination period for a householder planning application is 8 weeks from the date of validation. However, the total process including pre-application advice, design development, and submission preparation typically takes 3 to 4 months. If the application is refused and an appeal is lodged, the appeals process can add a further 6 to 12 months. ProStruct Design recommends seeking pre-application advice to increase the likelihood of approval.
