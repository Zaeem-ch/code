What Is a Velux Loft Conversion?

A Velux loft conversion, also known as a rooflight conversion, is the simplest and most cost-effective method of transforming an unused loft space into a habitable room. Unlike dormer or mansard conversions, a Velux conversion does not involve any structural changes to the external shape or profile of the roof. Instead, the existing roof structure is retained, and natural light is introduced through roof windows (commonly known by the brand name Velux, the market leader in rooflight products) that are installed within the plane of the existing roof slope.

The beauty of a Velux conversion lies in its simplicity. Because the roof form remains unchanged, the external appearance of the property is minimally affected, planning permission is almost never required, and the construction programme is significantly shorter than for other conversion types. The main works involve strengthening the floor structure, installing a staircase, fitting the rooflights, adding insulation between and below the rafters, providing fire protection measures, and completing the internal fit-out with plasterboard, electrics, plumbing, and decoration.

At ProStruct Design, we prepare the complete set of architectural and structural drawings required for a Velux loft conversion, including floor plans, sections, structural calculations, and building regulations submissions. Even though a rooflight conversion is the simplest type, it still requires carefully prepared drawings to obtain building regulations approval and to ensure the finished result is safe, comfortable, and compliant.

Is Your Loft Suitable for a Velux Conversion?

The suitability of a loft for a Velux-only conversion depends primarily on the existing headroom. Because no external structural changes are made to increase the volume of the roof space, the loft must already have sufficient height between the floor and the ridge to create a comfortable habitable room. The critical measurement is from the top of the existing ceiling joists to the underside of the ridge board at the highest point of the roof.

As a general rule, you need a minimum of 2.2 metres at the ridge to make a Velux conversion viable. Once the floor build-up is added (typically 25mm of boarding plus acoustic insulation), the finished floor-to-ceiling height at the centre of the room will be around 2.1 to 2.15 metres, which is adequate for a bedroom or study. The usable area extends outward from the ridge towards the eaves, with the sloping ceilings limiting the functional space to areas where the headroom exceeds about 1.5 metres.

Properties that are good candidates for Velux conversions include houses with steep roof pitches (typically 40 degrees or more), which provide more headroom across a wider floor area. Victorian houses with their characteristically steep roof slopes often work well, as do many 1930s semi-detached properties. Bungalows with high-pitched roofs can also be excellent candidates, particularly where the roof space is generous.

If your loft measurement falls short of the 2.2-metre threshold, a Velux-only conversion may not be practical, and you should consider alternatives that increase the volume of the roof space. A rear dormer adds headroom along the back of the property, while a hip to gable conversion reclaims the volume lost to a hipped roof end. Our initial survey will measure your loft and advise on the most suitable conversion type for your property.

Planning Permission for Velux Conversions

One of the most attractive features of a Velux loft conversion is that it almost never requires planning permission. Since the roof shape is not altered, no extensions are constructed, and the rooflights sit within the existing plane of the roof, the works are classified as a material alteration rather than a development requiring planning consent. This means there is no planning application fee, no 8-week waiting period for a decision, and no risk of refusal.

The permitted development rules do impose some conditions on rooflights. Windows on a side elevation that face a neighbouring property must be obscure-glazed and non-opening if they are less than 1.7 metres above the finished floor level. This is to protect the privacy of adjacent properties. On the front and rear elevations, there are no such restrictions, and rooflights can be clear-glazed and openable without constraint.

The exceptions to the no-planning-permission rule are limited but important. Listed buildings always require listed building consent for any alterations, including the installation of rooflights. Properties in conservation areas may need permission for rooflights on the front elevation if they would be visible from the public highway, depending on the specific Article 4 direction in force. And if your property has had its permitted development rights removed by a condition on a previous planning permission, you may need consent for any external works. We check the planning status of every property at the outset to confirm whether any permissions are needed.

Rooflight Sizing and Building Regulations Requirements

The size and number of rooflights in a Velux conversion are governed by the Building Regulations, specifically Part L (Conservation of Fuel and Power) and Part F (Ventilation). These regulations set minimum standards for daylight provision, ventilation, and thermal performance that our drawings are designed to satisfy.

Daylight: The 20% Rule

Part L of the Building Regulations requires that the glazed area in a habitable room must be at least 20% of the floor area of that room. For a typical loft bedroom of 15 square metres, this means a minimum of 3 square metres of glazed area. Since individual Velux windows range from about 0.5 to 1.5 square metres depending on the size chosen, most loft conversions require between 2 and 4 rooflights to meet the daylight standard. Our drawings specify the exact window sizes and positions to ensure compliance, taking account of the available roof area, rafter spacings, and the homeowner's preferences for natural light.

Ventilation Requirements

Part F requires that habitable rooms have both purge ventilation (the ability to rapidly ventilate the room by opening a window) and background ventilation (continuous low-level airflow to prevent condensation and maintain air quality). At least one openable rooflight per room provides purge ventilation, while trickle vents integrated into the window frame provide background ventilation. Bathrooms and en-suites in the loft require mechanical extract ventilation, typically via a fan ducted to an external outlet through the roof.

Part L Energy Compliance

Rooflights must meet minimum thermal performance standards to limit heat loss through the roof. The maximum U-value for rooflights in a loft conversion is 1.6 W/m2K, although modern Velux windows typically achieve values of 1.0 to 1.3 W/m2K with double glazing and 0.8 to 1.0 W/m2K with triple glazing. The roof construction around the windows must also be insulated to achieve an overall roof U-value of 0.18 W/m2K, and the junction between the window frame and the surrounding insulation must be carefully detailed to avoid thermal bridging.

Floor Structure and Staircase Design

Although the roof structure remains unchanged in a Velux conversion, the floor and staircase elements require the same level of design attention as any other loft conversion type. The existing ceiling joists were designed to support only the weight of the ceiling plasterboard below and the lightweight loft insulation above. They are not adequate to carry the imposed loads of a habitable room, which include people, furniture, and stored items.

The standard approach is to install new floor joists alongside or between the existing ceiling joists. These new joists are typically 225mm x 50mm or 47mm C24 grade timber at 400mm centres, although the exact specification depends on the span and loading requirements calculated by the structural engineer. In some cases, engineered timber I-joists or metal web joists are used because they offer greater depth for a given weight, allow services to be routed through the web, and can span longer distances without intermediate support.

The staircase design is often the most challenging aspect of a Velux conversion because the limited headroom under the existing roof slope constrains where the staircase can be positioned. Our architectural drawings show the staircase layout in detail, demonstrating that the minimum headroom of 2.0 metres is achieved over the entire length of the stair, and that the pitch does not exceed 42 degrees. We explore multiple staircase options during the design stage to find the arrangement that minimises the impact on the floor below while providing safe and comfortable access to the new loft rooms.

Insulation and Thermal Performance

Insulation is a critical component of any Velux loft conversion because the roof is the primary thermal boundary between the heated living space and the external environment. Without proper insulation, the loft rooms would be uncomfortably hot in summer and cold in winter, and the energy consumption for heating would be significantly higher than necessary.

The most common insulation approach for a Velux conversion is to insulate between and below the rafters. The existing rafters (typically 100mm or 125mm deep) are packed with mineral wool or rigid PIR insulation, and additional insulation is then fixed to the underside of the rafters on battens to achieve the required total thickness. The target U-value for the roof is 0.18 W/m2K, which typically requires 150mm to 200mm of insulation depending on the product chosen. A 25mm to 50mm ventilation gap must be maintained between the top of the insulation and the underside of the roof felt or breathable membrane to prevent condensation.

A vapour control layer (VCL) is installed on the warm side of the insulation, between the insulation and the internal plasterboard, to prevent warm moist air from the living space penetrating into the insulation layer where it could condense. The VCL must be carefully sealed at all joints, junctions, and penetrations to be effective. Our section drawings show the complete insulation and VCL build-up, with details at the eaves, ridge, and around the rooflight openings.

Costs for Velux Loft Conversions

A Velux loft conversion is the most affordable type of loft conversion, with costs in London typically ranging from £20,000 to £35,000. The lower cost compared to dormer and mansard conversions reflects the simpler construction process: no structural changes to the roof, reduced scaffolding requirements, shorter build times, and less complex engineering.

At the entry level, a basic Velux conversion creating a single bedroom with 2 to 3 rooflights, a standard staircase, and a simple finish will cost around £20,000 to £25,000. A more comprehensive scheme with a bedroom and en-suite shower room, 3 to 4 rooflights including larger sizes, quality finishes, and integrated storage will typically fall between £25,000 and £35,000. The upper end of the range applies to properties where the floor structure requires significant reinforcement, where the staircase design is complex, or where high-specification fixtures and fittings are chosen.

Our professional fees for the architectural and structural drawing package for a Velux conversion start from £1,800 to £2,500. This includes the measured survey, design development, structural engineering coordination, and building regulations application. The building control fees are payable separately to the local authority or approved inspector and are typically between £300 and £600 for a straightforward rooflight conversion.

Frequently Asked Questions About Velux Loft Conversions

Does a Velux loft conversion need planning permission?

In the vast majority of cases, a Velux loft conversion does not require planning permission. Since the roof shape is not altered and no extensions are added, the works typically fall within permitted development rights. The main exceptions are properties in conservation areas where rooflights on the front elevation may need consent, listed buildings which always need listed building consent, and properties subject to Article 4 directions.

What is the cheapest type of loft conversion?

The Velux or rooflight loft conversion is the cheapest type, typically costing between £20,000 and £35,000 in London. It is more affordable than other types because no structural changes are made to the roof shape. The existing roof structure is retained, and natural light is provided by installing roof windows rather than constructing dormers. The savings come from reduced structural work, shorter build times, and simpler scaffolding requirements.

How much headroom do I need for a Velux loft conversion?

You need a minimum of 2.2 metres headroom from the top of the existing ceiling joists to the underside of the ridge board for a Velux loft conversion to be viable. This measurement is taken before the floor build-up is added, so the actual finished floor-to-ceiling height will be slightly less. Properties with lower ridge heights may not be suitable for a rooflight-only conversion and should consider a dormer or mansard alternative instead.

How many Velux windows do I need in a loft conversion?

Building regulations require that the glazed area of rooflights must equal at least 20% of the floor area of the room being created. For a typical 15 square metre bedroom, this means a minimum of 3 square metres of glazed area. Most loft conversions incorporate 2 to 4 Velux windows depending on the room size and roof configuration. Additional windows may be desired for ventilation purposes, as at least one openable window per room is required for background ventilation.

What size Velux windows should I choose for a loft conversion?

The most popular Velux window sizes for loft conversions are the MK08 (780mm x 1400mm) and the SK06 (1140mm x 1180mm). For rooms where maximum daylight is desired, the larger FK06 (660mm x 1180mm) or UK04 (1340mm x 980mm) sizes are available. The choice depends on the rafter spacing, the available roof area between structural timbers, and the aesthetic preferences of the homeowner. Our drawings specify the exact window sizes and positions for your project.

Is a Velux loft conversion worth it compared to a dormer?

A Velux conversion is worth it if your loft already has good headroom and you want to keep costs down and minimise disruption. It costs roughly half the price of a dormer conversion and can be completed in 4 to 6 weeks rather than 8 to 12 weeks. However, it provides less usable floor space because the sloping ceilings are retained. If you need maximum space, a dormer or mansard conversion will provide significantly more habitable area.
