Understanding Hip to Gable Loft Conversions

A hip to gable loft conversion is a specialist roof alteration that replaces the sloping hip end of a roof with a vertical gable wall, dramatically increasing the usable volume within the loft space. In a standard hipped roof, one or both sides of the roof slope inward from the eaves to meet the ridge at a reduced length. This attractive roof form, common on 1930s semi-detached houses and post-war detached properties, unfortunately wastes a significant amount of potential living space because the sloping hip reduces the headroom across a large portion of the loft floor.

By extending the ridge beam outward to the new gable wall and building a vertical brick or blockwork end wall in place of the hip, the conversion reclaims this lost volume. The result is a loft space that has full headroom right out to the side wall of the property, where previously the ceiling would have tapered down to an unusable point. This transformation is particularly valuable in areas such as Wembley and Harrow, where the 1930s semi-detached housing stock is ideally suited to this type of conversion.

Which Properties Suit a Hip to Gable Conversion?

Hip to gable conversions are best suited to semi-detached and detached houses that have at least one hipped end to the roof. The most common candidates are the classic 1930s semi-detached properties found throughout suburban London, which were built in enormous numbers during the inter-war building boom. These houses typically feature a half-hipped or fully-hipped roof on the exposed side, with a gable or party wall on the shared side.

Detached houses with hips on both sides can benefit from a double hip to gable conversion, where both ends of the roof are squared off. This creates an even greater volume gain but requires more extensive structural work and may have a bigger impact on the external appearance. For detached properties, the permitted development volume allowance of 50 cubic metres provides adequate scope for a double hip to gable in most cases.

End-of-terrace houses with a hipped end are also excellent candidates. While mid-terrace properties already have gable walls on both sides, end-of-terrace homes often have a hip on the exposed flank. Converting this hip to a gable can transform the loft space from a cramped triangular area into a generous room with full standing headroom throughout.

Properties that are less suitable include those with very low ridge heights, where even with the hip removed there would be insufficient headroom for a habitable room. Similarly, houses where the hip forms part of a complex roof geometry with multiple ridges and valleys may present challenges that make a hip to gable conversion impractical or disproportionately expensive. Our initial survey will assess your property and confirm whether this conversion type is the right approach.

Permitted Development Rules for Hip to Gable Conversions

Many hip to gable conversions can be carried out under permitted development (PD) rights, avoiding the need for a formal planning application. The relevant legislation is Schedule 2, Part 1, Class B and Class C of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015, which allows for enlargements to the roof of a dwellinghouse.

The critical volumetric limits apply to all roof enlargements, including the hip to gable alteration and any associated rear dormer. For detached houses, the combined volume of all roof enlargements must not exceed 50 cubic metres. For semi-detached and end-of-terrace houses, the limit is 40 cubic metres. These volumes are measured externally and include any previous roof enlargements carried out since the original construction of the property (or since 1 July 1948, if the house was built before that date).

Additional PD conditions require that the hip to gable alteration must not extend above the height of the existing roof ridge. The new gable wall must be constructed in materials that are similar in appearance to the existing external walls of the property. No part of the enlargement can extend beyond the outer face of the original side wall by more than a specified distance, and the works must not result in a roof extension that overhangs the outer face of the wall of the original house.

It is important to note that permitted development rights can be removed or restricted in certain areas. Properties in conservation areas, national parks, the Broads, Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty, or World Heritage Sites have reduced PD rights for roof alterations. Article 4 directions imposed by the local authority can also remove specific PD rights. We always recommend obtaining a Lawful Development Certificate from the local council to confirm that PD rights apply before commencing work. This provides legal certainty and is a valuable document when selling the property in future.

Structural Steel Requirements

The structural engineering for a hip to gable conversion is more complex than for a straightforward dormer loft conversion because the fundamental geometry of the roof structure is being altered. Removing the hip rafters eliminates a key structural element that previously braced the roof and transferred loads to the supporting walls. This must be replaced with an engineered solution that maintains the structural integrity of the roof while creating the new gable end.

The most significant structural element in a hip to gable conversion is the new ridge beam. In the original hipped roof, the ridge board is a relatively lightweight timber member that acts primarily as a positioning guide for the rafters. When the hip is removed and the ridge is extended to the new gable wall, this member must be replaced with a structural ridge beam, typically a universal steel beam (UB), that spans from the existing roof structure to the new gable. This beam carries the weight of the rafters on both sides and transfers the loads to the supporting walls at each end.

A steel beam is also typically required at the head of the new gable wall, forming a ring beam or wall plate detail that distributes the roof loads evenly along the top of the new brickwork. The new gable wall itself must be designed to resist wind loads and to support the roof structure, which often requires a reinforced blockwork inner leaf with stainless steel wall ties to the outer brick leaf.

The floor structure requires careful attention as well. New steel beams or engineered timber joists are specified to support the loft floor, and these must be designed to carry the imposed loads of a habitable room (typically 1.5 kN/m2 for bedrooms) plus the dead loads of the floor construction, ceiling below, and partition walls. Our structural engineers calculate beam sizes, connection details, and bearing lengths for every steel element, ensuring the design meets the requirements of BS EN 1993 for steelwork and BS EN 1996 for masonry.

Combining Hip to Gable with a Rear Dormer

The most popular and effective approach for hip to gable conversions is to combine the gable end alteration with a rear dormer, creating an L-shaped loft conversion that maximises the available space. This combination works so well because the two elements complement each other: the hip to gable provides full headroom across the width at one end of the loft, while the rear dormer extends the usable area along the back of the property.

The L-shaped layout typically allows for a generous master bedroom at the gable end, with an en-suite bathroom positioned within the dormer section, and a separate study, dressing room, or additional bedroom alongside. The staircase is usually positioned towards the centre of the loft, rising from the existing landing below, with the layout designed to minimise the impact on the existing first-floor accommodation.

From a structural perspective, the combined scheme requires careful coordination between the gable steelwork and the dormer steelwork. The rear dormer beam typically runs at right angles to the ridge beam, and the connection between these two primary structural elements must be detailed to transfer loads safely. Our drawing packages show the complete steelwork layout in plan, with connection details at every junction and beam-to-beam interface.

The volumetric allowance under permitted development must accommodate both the hip to gable and the rear dormer combined. For a typical semi-detached house, the 40 cubic metre limit is sufficient for a modest L-shaped conversion, but the design must be carefully sized to stay within the allowance. We calculate the volumes precisely during the design stage and can advise on the maximum dormer size that can be achieved within PD limits. Where a larger scheme is desired, a full planning application can be submitted to the local authority.

New Gable Wall Construction: Brickwork and Cavity Insulation

The new gable wall is one of the most visible elements of a hip to gable conversion, and getting the construction details right is essential for both structural performance and aesthetic quality. The wall is typically constructed as a cavity wall with an outer leaf of facing brickwork to match the existing property and an inner leaf of dense concrete blockwork providing structural support.

Matching the brickwork is critical for a successful outcome. The bricks used for the new gable should be as close a match as possible to the existing bricks in terms of colour, texture, size, and bond pattern. For older properties where the original bricks are no longer manufactured, reclaimed bricks from specialist suppliers may be needed. Our drawings specify the brick type, mortar colour, and bond pattern to guide the bricklayer, and we recommend that homeowners obtain sample panels before the full wall is built to approve the match.

The cavity between the two leaves is typically 100mm wide and is filled with full-fill cavity insulation to meet the thermal performance requirements of Part L of the Building Regulations. Mineral wool batts or blown bead insulation are commonly used, and the U-value target for a new external wall is 0.28 W/m2K. Stainless steel cavity wall ties at specified centres connect the two leaves structurally, and a damp-proof course is installed at the base of the wall to prevent moisture rising.

At the junction between the new gable wall and the existing roof, careful weathering details are required. Lead flashings, cavity trays, and weep holes ensure that any water penetrating the outer leaf is safely directed back to the exterior. Our detail drawings at 1:5 and 1:10 scale show these critical junctions in full, with material specifications and fixing instructions for the construction team.

Costs for Hip to Gable Loft Conversions

A hip to gable loft conversion in London typically costs between £35,000 and £65,000, with the variation reflecting the scope of the project, the extent of structural work, and the level of internal finish specified. A straightforward single hip to gable without a rear dormer will fall at the lower end of this range, while a combined hip to gable with full-width rear dormer, high-specification bathroom, and premium finishes will approach the upper end.

The main cost components are the structural steelwork and brickwork for the new gable wall (which can account for £8,000 to £15,000 of the total), the rear dormer construction if included, the new floor structure and staircase, the bathroom installation, and the internal fit-out including plastering, electrics, plumbing, and decoration. Scaffolding costs are typically higher for hip to gable conversions than for simple dormers because access is needed to the gable end as well as the rear.

Our professional fees for the complete architectural and structural drawing package for a hip to gable conversion range from £3,000 to £5,000, depending on the complexity. This includes the measured survey, feasibility assessment, architectural design, structural engineering, and building regulations application. The building control application fee payable to the local authority is separate and varies by borough, but is typically between £400 and £800 for a loft conversion.

Frequently Asked Questions About Hip to Gable Conversions

What is a hip to gable loft conversion?

A hip to gable loft conversion involves changing the sloping side of a hipped roof (where the roof slopes inward at the side) into a vertical gable wall. This creates a flat, upright wall where the hip previously sloped away, significantly increasing the usable internal floor area and headroom. It is one of the most effective ways to maximise space in semi-detached and detached properties.

Is a hip to gable conversion permitted development?

In many cases, a hip to gable conversion can be carried out under permitted development rights without a formal planning application. The key restrictions are that the new gable wall must not extend beyond the existing roof ridge height, the total volume added must not exceed 50 cubic metres for detached houses or 40 cubic metres for terraced properties, and the property must not be in a conservation area or subject to Article 4 directions.

How much does a hip to gable loft conversion cost?

A hip to gable loft conversion in London typically costs between £35,000 and £65,000. The variation depends on whether the conversion includes a rear dormer extension, the extent of structural steelwork required, the internal specification, and the complexity of the brickwork and cavity construction for the new gable wall. A combined hip to gable with rear dormer is the most popular option and usually costs between £45,000 and £60,000.

Can you do a hip to gable conversion on a terraced house?

Hip to gable conversions are not typically suitable for mid-terrace properties because terraced houses usually have gable walls on both sides already. However, end-of-terrace properties with a hipped roof on the exposed side can benefit from a hip to gable conversion. The volume allowance for terraced properties is 40 cubic metres compared to 50 cubic metres for detached homes.

What structural steelwork is needed for a hip to gable conversion?

A hip to gable conversion requires significant structural steelwork. The existing hip rafters must be removed and replaced with a new ridge board or ridge beam extending to the new gable wall. A steel beam is typically installed along the line of the new gable to support the extended roof structure. Additional steels may be needed to support the new floor joists and to reinforce the party wall or existing side wall where loads are transferred.

Can a hip to gable be combined with a rear dormer?

Yes, combining a hip to gable with a rear dormer is the most popular approach and creates an L-shaped loft conversion that maximises the available space. The gable end provides full headroom across the width of the property at one end, while the rear dormer extends the usable area along the back. This combination is ideal for creating a large master bedroom with en-suite bathroom, or multiple rooms on the new loft floor.
