What Is a Rear Dormer Loft Conversion?

A rear dormer loft conversion is the single most popular type of loft conversion carried out in London. It involves constructing a dormer extension that projects outward from the rear slope of the roof, adding substantial headroom and floor area to the loft space while leaving the front of the property completely unaltered. Because the dormer faces the garden rather than the street, it has minimal visual impact on the public realm, which is precisely why rear dormers benefit from generous permitted development allowances that avoid the need for planning permission in most cases.

The rear dormer has become the go-to solution for London homeowners seeking to add bedrooms, bathrooms, and living space to their properties without the cost and complexity of a ground-floor extension or a full mansard conversion. The appeal is clear: a rear dormer can transform a cramped, unusable loft into a bright, spacious room with full-height walls along the back of the property, all within a relatively modest budget and a construction programme that typically takes just 8 to 10 weeks.

Permitted Development Rules for Rear Dormers

The majority of rear dormers on houses (not flats or maisonettes) can be built under permitted development (PD) rights without the need for a planning application. This is governed by Schedule 2, Part 1, Class B of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015 (as amended). The PD rules set out specific conditions that the dormer must satisfy.

Volumetric Limits

The total volume of all roof enlargements, measured externally, must not exceed 40 cubic metres for terraced houses (including end-of-terrace) or 50 cubic metres for semi-detached and detached houses. These limits include the proposed dormer and any previous roof enlargements carried out since the original construction (or since 1 July 1948 for older properties). A full-width rear dormer on a typical London terrace normally uses between 25 and 35 cubic metres of the allowance, leaving scope for the project to fit comfortably within the 40 cubic metre limit.

Set-Back and Positioning Rules

The dormer must be set back at least 200mm from the original eaves line. This creates a visible step between the bottom of the dormer and the gutter line, which is a characteristic feature of PD dormers. The dormer must not extend above the highest part of the existing roof (the ridge line), and it must not project beyond the outer face of the original rear wall of the house at eaves level. Our drawings dimension these set-backs precisely to demonstrate PD compliance.

Materials

The materials used on the exterior of the dormer must be similar in appearance to the existing materials on the house. For the cheek walls (the side faces of the dormer), this typically means hanging tiles, render, or timber cladding that matches the existing elevations. The flat roof covering can be GRP fibreglass, single-ply membrane, or lead, as these are accepted as appropriate materials for a flat dormer roof regardless of the main roof material.

Restrictions

PD rights for rear dormers can be removed or restricted in several circumstances. Properties in conservation areas, national parks, the Broads, and Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty have limited PD rights for roof enlargements. Article 4 directions issued by the local authority can remove PD rights for specific types of development. Some planning permissions include conditions that restrict future PD rights. We always check the planning history and designations of a property before confirming that PD applies.

Box Dormer vs Pitched Dormer: Which to Choose?

When designing a rear dormer, the first major decision is whether to opt for a box dormer (flat-roof) or a pitched dormer (sloping roof). Each has distinct advantages, and the right choice depends on your priorities for space, appearance, and budget.

Box Dormers (Flat-Roof)

The box dormer is by far the most common choice for rear dormer conversions in London. Its rectangular profile with a flat roof maximises the internal volume, creating full-height walls right up to the back of the dormer where windows are positioned for natural light. The flat ceiling extends the entire depth of the dormer, providing consistent headroom that makes the room feel like a natural extension of the house rather than a converted attic space.

From a practical standpoint, box dormers are quicker and more straightforward to construct than pitched alternatives, which typically translates to lower construction costs. The flat roof can be finished in a range of durable, low-maintenance materials including GRP fibreglass (which offers a seamless, watertight surface), single-ply EPDM or TPO membranes, or traditional lead sheet for a premium finish. Our drawings specify a minimum roof fall of 1:40 to ensure positive drainage, with carefully positioned outlets and internal gutters where needed.

Pitched Dormers

A pitched dormer incorporates a sloping roof element that mirrors the angle and materials of the main house roof. This creates a more visually sympathetic addition that can blend with the existing roofscape, particularly when matching tiles or slates are used. Pitched dormers are occasionally preferred by homeowners who find the utilitarian appearance of a box dormer unappealing, or in areas where the local authority has indicated a preference for pitched roof forms in supplementary planning guidance.

The trade-off is a reduction in internal headroom at the rear of the dormer. Where a box dormer provides a flat ceiling at full height throughout, a pitched dormer has a sloping ceiling that reduces the headroom as it rises towards the ridge of the dormer. This means slightly less usable floor area overall, particularly at the rear wall where the window is located. However, the difference can be minimised through careful design of the dormer proportions.

Structural Support for Rear Dormers

Building a rear dormer requires careful structural design to ensure the new structure is properly supported and that the loads are safely transferred through the existing building. When a section of the rear roof slope is cut away to form the dormer opening, the remaining roof structure on either side must be reinforced, and new structural elements must be introduced to carry the weight of the dormer walls, roof, and glazing.

The primary structural element is the dormer beam, which spans across the top of the dormer opening at the junction with the retained roof slope. This beam, typically a universal steel beam (UB), carries the weight of the dormer cheek walls, the flat roof structure, and any snow or wind loads acting on the roof. For a full-width dormer spanning 5 to 6 metres, a beam size of 152x89 or 178x102 UB is common, though the exact size depends on the structural engineer's calculations for the specific loading conditions.

Trimmer rafters are installed on each side of the dormer opening to form the cheek wall positions. These are typically doubled-up rafters that carry the additional loads from the dormer structure and transfer them to the wall plate at the eaves. Where the dormer extends close to the party wall or side wall, the loads may be transferred directly into the masonry via a padstone bearing detail.

The floor structure must also be designed for the increased loads. New joists or steel beams are specified alongside or in place of the original ceiling joists, designed to carry the 1.5 kN/m2 imposed load of a bedroom plus the dead loads of the floor and ceiling construction. Our structural drawings show every beam, joist, and connection in detail, with sizes, grades, and fixing specifications clearly annotated.

Rear Dormers on London Terraced Houses

London's Victorian and Edwardian terraced houses are the ideal candidates for rear dormer conversions. These properties, which make up a significant proportion of the capital's housing stock, typically feature a uniform rear roofline that faces the garden, providing an unobtrusive location for a dormer addition. The consistent architectural style of terraced streets means that well-designed rear dormers have become an accepted and expected feature of London's residential landscape.

A full-width rear dormer on a typical London terrace creates approximately 12 to 18 square metres of additional habitable floor space, depending on the width and depth of the property. This is sufficient for a generous master bedroom with en-suite bathroom, or for two smaller rooms such as a bedroom and a study. The dormer windows overlook the garden, providing natural light and pleasant views that enhance the quality of the living space.

Areas such as Hammersmith and Fulham, where Victorian terraces are predominant, have seen enormous numbers of rear dormer conversions over the past two decades. Similarly, the terraced streets of Islington, Hackney, Wandsworth, and Lambeth are dotted with rear dormers that have become a natural part of the urban roofscape. In these established contexts, a new rear dormer that follows the prevailing design language is readily accepted under permitted development rights.

Building Regulations for Rear Dormers

Regardless of whether planning permission is required, all rear dormer loft conversions must comply with the Building Regulations. A building regulations application must be submitted and approved before construction begins, and the completed works must be inspected and signed off by a building control surveyor. The main regulatory requirements are similar to those for other loft conversion types but with specific considerations relevant to dormer construction.

Part A (Structure) requires that the dormer structure and the supporting elements below are designed to resist all applied loads safely. Part B (Fire Safety) mandates a protected escape route from the loft to the final exit door, typically achieved through FD30 fire doors and fire-resistant linings to the staircase enclosure. Interlinked smoke alarms must be installed on every level of the house, including the new loft floor.

Part L (Conservation of Fuel and Power) sets thermal performance targets for the dormer walls (U-value 0.28 W/m2K), the flat roof (0.18 W/m2K), and any new windows (1.6 W/m2K for rooflights, 1.4 W/m2K for vertical windows in the dormer face). Part E (Sound Insulation) requires adequate acoustic separation between the new loft rooms and the rooms below, and between the loft and any adjoining properties sharing a party wall. Our drawings specify the complete construction build-up for every element, with insulation types, thicknesses, and U-value calculations annotated on the section drawings.

Costs for Rear Dormer Loft Conversions

Rear dormer loft conversions offer excellent value, with costs in London typically ranging from £25,000 to £45,000. This is less than a hip to gable or mansard conversion because the structural work is more contained and the construction process is faster.

A single rear dormer creating one bedroom on a terraced house, with a straightforward staircase route and basic finish, will typically cost between £25,000 and £30,000. A full-width rear dormer with a bedroom and en-suite bathroom, quality fixtures, and a mid-range finish specification will fall in the £30,000 to £40,000 range. Higher specifications, complex staircase arrangements, or difficult site access can push costs towards £45,000.

Frequently Asked Questions About Rear Dormer Conversions

Is a rear dormer permitted development?

Yes, most rear dormers on houses fall within permitted development rights and do not require a planning application. The dormer must be set back at least 200mm from the original eaves, must not extend above the highest part of the roof, and must not exceed the volumetric limits of 40 cubic metres for terraced houses or 50 cubic metres for semi-detached and detached houses. Properties in conservation areas or subject to Article 4 directions may have these rights restricted.

What is the maximum size for a rear dormer under permitted development?

Under permitted development, a rear dormer must not exceed 40 cubic metres in volume for terraced houses or 50 cubic metres for semi-detached and detached houses. The dormer must be set back at least 200mm from the original eaves line and must not extend above the highest part of the existing roof. It must not project beyond the outer face of the original rear wall. These volume limits include any previous roof enlargements.

What is the difference between a box dormer and a pitched dormer?

A box dormer has a flat roof and vertical walls, creating a rectangular box-shaped projection from the roof slope. It maximises the internal volume and headroom. A pitched dormer has a sloping roof that echoes the pitch of the main roof, creating a more traditional appearance. Box dormers provide more internal space and are more common at the rear of properties, while pitched dormers are preferred for front elevations or conservation areas where visual impact is a consideration.

How much does a rear dormer loft conversion cost?

A rear dormer loft conversion in London typically costs between £25,000 and £45,000. A single dormer creating one bedroom will be at the lower end, while a full-width rear dormer spanning the entire property width with a bedroom and en-suite bathroom will be towards the upper end. The cost includes architectural drawings, structural engineering, building control fees, construction, and standard internal fit-out.

Do the materials of a rear dormer need to match the house?

Under permitted development rules, the materials used on the exterior of a rear dormer should be similar in appearance to the existing materials on the house. For the cheek walls this typically means hanging tiles or render to match, and for a flat-roof dormer the roof covering can be GRP fibreglass, single-ply membrane, or lead. However, the PD rules say "similar in appearance" rather than "identical", which allows some flexibility in material choice.

Can I build a full-width rear dormer on a terraced house?

Yes, a full-width rear dormer that spans the entire width of a terraced house is possible and very common in London. The dormer must comply with the permitted development volume limit of 40 cubic metres, must be set back from the eaves and set in from the party walls, and must not extend above the ridge. Full-width rear dormers on London terraces are one of the most popular loft conversion approaches, creating a generous open-plan loft space.
